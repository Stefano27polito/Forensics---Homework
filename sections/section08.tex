\begin{questionbox}[title=Question (b)]
All of the evidence in Sections 5--6 was provided to you as an export generated by the cloud
provider's own tooling. You have no way to physically verify that this export was not altered
before being handed to you.
\\
The artefact set includes \texttt{cloudtrail\_digest.json}, which is the provider's own answer to this problem. In production, CloudTrail emits a digest file for each interval containing the SHA-256 hash of every log file in that interval, the hash of the preceding digest, so that digests form a chain in which altering one entry invalidates every later one, and a signature over that content made with AWS's private key, verifiable by anyone holding the corresponding public key. The file supplied here reproduces the hashes and the chain reference, but carries no signature, because producing a valid one would require the provider's private key.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item Inspect the digest file and verify what can be verified: recompute the SHA-256 of
	      \texttt{cloudtrail\_events.json} and compare it against the recorded value. State
	      precisely what agreement between the two establishes, and what it leaves open.
	\item Why can reliance on a provider-produced digest be challenged in court, even when the
	      provider acted in good faith and the signature \emph{is} present and valid? Consider who
	      generated the material being attested, and at what point in its lifetime the attestation
	      was applied.
	\item Based on principles already covered in this course (hashing, chain of custody,
	      repeatability): given what you do \emph{not} control in a cloud environment, can
	      provider-mediated evidence like this one ever meet the same evidentiary standard as
	      evidence you acquired directly yourself? Justify your answer.
\end{enumerate}
